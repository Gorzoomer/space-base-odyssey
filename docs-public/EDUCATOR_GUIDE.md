# Space Base Odyssey - Educator guide

A short pack for teachers and club leaders who want to use Space Base Odyssey in a lesson. It has three 20-minute lesson plans, learning goals, discussion questions, how to run the game in class and safety notes. A Spanish version is in [GUIA_DOCENTE.md](GUIA_DOCENTE.md).

Space Base Odyssey is a Roblox game made for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 ("Build a Junior Astronaut Mission Trainer"). Kids aged 7 to 12 run a base at the Moon's south pole and learn that every choice costs something. It plays in English and Mexican Spanish, and every gameplay sentence has at most eight simple words.

The game is a team project, not a NASA product. Its storms replay real solar flares recorded by NASA's DONKI database, labelled as replays with their real date: they were observed near Earth, never at a Moon base. The game asks "what if a storm like this reached your base?".

## Learning goals

After playing, a child should be able to say these ideas in their own words:

1. **Power is limited.** There is never enough for everything, so you choose, and the choice shows up later.
2. **Air comes from water.** Astronauts make oxygen from water with electricity. No water or no power, less air.
3. **Plants take time.** Food you plant today feeds you tomorrow, not now.
4. **Thick walls protect you, and radiation adds up.** A shelter protects by its mass, not by power. Dose never goes away, so you hide when Sun particles come.
5. **A big flare does not always bring a particle storm,** and sheltering is still the right call.
6. **Data has a date.** A replay of a recorded flare is not live news.

## Before class

- **Accounts.** Roblox needs an account and the Roblox app. New Roblox experiences only open to age-checked accounts aged 16 or older until they pass Roblox's evaluation for all ages, so younger students may not be able to join the public game yet. In that case, play on a teacher account on a projector and let students decide together, or show the project's playthrough video.
- **Devices.** PC or tablet. Headphones help: the game has music and sound, and the classroom stays calm.
- **Language.** Settings, Game page: English or Spanish. The choice is saved with each account.
- **Helpers for young readers.** Settings, Helpers page: **Text size** Big, **Message time** Long or Longer (C.A.R.L.'s messages stay 1.5 or 2 times longer), and **Less motion** for students who feel unwell with camera movement. C.A.R.L.'s replay button next to its speech bubble shows the last 10 lines again.
- **Prepare a world.** A new world starts almost empty. In the game's pacing tests, a typical player reaches the greenhouse and the "Share the power" mission in about 12 minutes, the antenna and the "Base chores" mission in about 16 minutes, the storm shelter and the "Storm drill" in about 22 minutes, the first automatic ice drill in about 24 minutes and the cargo robot in about an hour. These are simulated times, so real players vary. Play your own world ahead of time to show later parts on a projector, or run the lessons in order over several sessions in the same worlds.

## Running it in class

- **Solo worlds (recommended for most lessons).** Each student picks a world, then **Just me**. Every world runs in its own private server, and nobody else can enter a Just me world.
- **With friends (groups of up to 4).** One student picks **With friends**; up to 3 classmates join through **Join a friend** on the start menu. Only the owner's Roblox friends can join, so the accounts must be Roblox friends first. The base belongs to the owner; each guest keeps their own credits, level and quests in their own save. If the owner leaves, the world closes and the guests return to the menu with their saves kept.
- **Missions.** Missions start from the **Missions** button or the mission console in the habitat. A mission day has a calm planning phase with no clock, a 150-second shift outside, and a report that explains what happened and why. Training missions never fail. Nobody gets hurt: a mission that goes wrong ends early, safely, with a reason.
- **Saving is automatic.** The game saves by itself; "Continue" on the start menu loads the last world. Nothing is lost if the bell rings.
- **Roles in groups.** With friends, suggest jobs: one student cleans dusty panels, one carries ice, one watches the antenna, and everyone runs to the shelter together.

## Lesson 1: Power trade-offs (20 minutes)

**Goal.** Power is limited; every choice has a cost that may show up later (goals 1 and 3).

**Game part.** The training mission **Share the power** (after building the greenhouse), then a day of **Chapter 1: New home**.

| Time | What happens |
| :--- | :--- |
| 0-4 min | Unplugged warm-up. Give each group 4 tokens and 3 cups labelled Air, Food and Science, with a rule: at most 2 tokens per cup. Ask them to share the tokens. Nobody can fill everything. |
| 4-14 min | Play. On the power board, students share the day's power cells between the air maker, the greenhouse and the science lab. Each clean, sunlit solar array gives one cell, plus one from the battery; a system takes at most 2. Ask them to predict, before the shift, which meter will drop. |
| 14-20 min | Discuss with the report on screen. |

**What to look for.** Greenhouse power today becomes food tomorrow, not today. Dust on a panel takes a cell away until someone cleans it. Science only counts once the antenna sends it to Earth.

**Discussion questions.**
- What did you give up to get more science?
- Why did the food go down the day after, not the same day?
- A dusty panel gave less power. What would you do first tomorrow?
- Real Moon bases may use solar power. Why is the Moon's south pole a good place for it?

**Real-world references.** A spot on the Shackleton crater rim is sunlit about 240 days a year (Mazarico et al., 2011, using LRO data). The first lettuce eaten on the Space Station grew for 33 days (NASA Veggie).

## Lesson 2: Radiation and the shelter (20 minutes)

**Goal.** The shelter protects by mass, not power; dose never goes down; a big flare does not always bring a particle storm (goals 4, 5 and 6).

**Game part.** The training mission **Storm drill** (after building the storm shelter). Optional: a storm day in **Chapter 2: Storm season**.

| Time | What happens |
| :--- | :--- |
| 0-4 min | Warm-up question: "What makes a good shield: a thick, heavy wall, or a machine with lots of power?" Take a show of hands and keep the answers. |
| 4-14 min | Play the Storm drill. A flare is spotted, C.A.R.L. names its real class and date, and the crew runs to the regolith shelter. Ask some students to stay outside for a few seconds on purpose and watch the radiation meter. |
| 14-20 min | Discuss. Show the storm card: "Replay of a real Sun flare from (date)". |

**What to look for.** The radiation meter climbs for anyone outside while particles are here and does not move for a crew in the shelter. It never goes back down. With the antenna linked, Earth warns the crew 45 seconds ahead; without it, only 15 (game values). Some storm days in the story are real false alarms: a huge flare with no particle storm.

**Discussion questions.**
- Why did the radiation meter not go down after the storm?
- The shelter uses no power. Why does a mound of Moon dirt protect you?
- The flare came, but no particles came. Was hiding still a good idea? Why?
- The game says "what if a storm like this reached your base". Where was the real flare seen?
- Why does a warning from Earth help?

**Real-world references.** NASA's radiation guidance (NASA-STD-3001 and NASA OCHMO) describes a storm shelter of about 10 cm of water equivalent. China's Chang'e-4 lander measured about 1.37 mSv a day on the Moon's surface, while the average person in the United States gets about 6.2 mSv a year (US EPA). The fastest Sun particles can reach Earth in about 30 minutes or less (NASA "Solar storms and flares"). The X9.0 flare of 3 October 2024 brought no particle storm near Earth (NASA / CCMC DONKI).

## Lesson 3: Ice to water to air (20 minutes)

**Goal.** Oxygen comes from water plus electricity; ice in dark polar craters can become water and air (goal 2).

**Game part.** The training mission **Base chores** (drill ice in the shadowed crater and deliver it to the water machine), and the base's **ice line**: drills, a belt, the silo, the water plant and, after the cargo robot is built, the air maker. If students have not built the ice line yet, show it from a prepared world on a projector.

| Time | What happens |
| :--- | :--- |
| 0-4 min | Draw the chain on the board: ice, then water, then air, with an arrow labelled "power" pointing at the last step. Ask: "What happens if the power goes away?" |
| 4-14 min | Play Base chores: drill ice in the crater and carry it to the water machine. Then visit the ice line. The water plant's sign says it in icons (ice, water, air) and in words: "Ice makes water. Water plus power makes air." |
| 14-20 min | Discuss. If you can, show the air maker's lamp: green while it makes air, amber without power (fewer than 2 solar arrays standing). Without power, the cargo robot brings water instead of air. |

**What to look for.** In a mission, ice adds air only when the air maker has power. On the ice line, air pays more than water, but only with enough solar panels. The drills keep digging while the owner is away, but only up to the silo's size.

**Discussion questions.**
- Where does the air in the base come from?
- What happened to the air maker without enough solar panels?
- Why would astronauts dig ice on the Moon instead of bringing all their water from Earth?
- The Space Station recycles almost all its water. Why does that matter for a crew far from home?

**Real-world references.** On the Space Station, oxygen is made by splitting water with electricity from the solar panels (NASA ECLSS). Each crew member uses about 0.9 kg of oxygen and 2.5 kg of drinking and food water a day (NASA BVAD, 2022). The LCROSS impact in 2009 found water ice in the shadowed Cabeus crater, about 5.6% of the soil by mass (Colaprete et al., Science, 2010). In 2023 the Space Station's life support showed it can recover 98% of its water (NASA).

## More discussion questions

- Which choice in the game was the hardest? What did it cost?
- Did anything happen later because of something you did earlier?
- What would you build first on a real Moon base, and why?
- How did your team share the jobs during a storm?
- C.A.R.L. said what worked ("More panels made more power."). What strategy worked for you?

## Safety notes for kids

- **Nobody gets hurt.** If suit air runs out, C.A.R.L. brings the astronaut inside. A mission that goes wrong ends early and safely, with an explanation.
- **Private by default.** A Just me world is the student's alone. In a With friends world, only the owner's Roblox friends can join, and at most 4 players.
- **Nothing to buy.** The game sells nothing. The daily supply capsule has a fixed calendar shown in advance, with no random rewards. It says "A game gift, not a real NASA flight."
- **No pressure to come back.** Missing a day takes nothing away ("Missed a day? Your calendar just waits."). There are no streaks. After 45 minutes of play, C.A.R.L. says once: "Base is safe. Great time for a break!".
- **Online behaviour.** Roblox's own chat and safety settings apply to each account; parents and schools can manage them in Roblox's parental controls. Remind students never to share personal details in a game.
- **Comfort.** The Less motion helper turns off camera shaking, confetti and screen flashes. The volume sliders in Settings control music and effects.
- **No links out.** The game has no links that leave it. Sources are credited as text.
